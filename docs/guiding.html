How Light Is Guided

A photonic chip moves light along tiny paths called waveguides, much like wires carry electricity. Four short steps explain why light stays inside them, and what happens when it doesn't.

CHAPTER 1 · HOW LIGHT IS GUIDEDTOOL 1 OF 7

Why does light stay inside a tiny strip of glass or silicon?

Key idea: Light is trapped by total internal reflection in a high-index core. As a wave, it can only travel in a few stable patterns, the modes.

You need: nothing: this is the starting point

Three things to try
  1. Find the critical angle for silicon against glass in step 1.
  2. In step 3, make the core thinner until only mode 0 is left.
  3. In step 4, compare bend losses for high and low index contrast.
STEP 1 OF 4

Light bends at a boundary

Light travels more slowly in glass or silicon than in air. How much slower is given by the refractive index n. When light crosses into a material with a different index, it changes direction.

This bending is described by Snell's law, which links the angle θ₁ in the first material to the angle θ₂ in the second. Drag the ray, or use the slider. Light starts in the denser material at the top, so the ray bends away from the perpendicular as it leaves.

Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

Now increase the angle. Because n1 is larger than n2, θ₂ grows faster than θ₁, until θ₂ reaches 90° and the refracted ray just skims along the boundary. Putting θ₂ = 90° into Snell's law gives the critical angle θc. Beyond it, Snell's law has no solution: no light can get out and every bit of it is reflected. This is total internal reflection.

θ₂ = 90° ⇒ sin θc = n₂ / n₁
STEP 2 OF 4

A core surrounded by cladding traps light

A waveguide is a thin layer or strip of high-index material, the core, surrounded by lower-index cladding. Light that hits the walls at a shallow enough angle is totally reflected every time and zig-zags along the core with no loss.

Steepen the launch angle. Once the light meets the wall more steeply than the critical angle allows, part of it escapes at every bounce, and the beam fades away. A larger index difference between core and cladding traps a much wider range of angles. That's why silicon on glass guides light so strongly.

STEP 3 OF 4

Light is a wave, so only certain patterns fit

The ray picture misses something. Light is a wave, and the up-going and down-going parts of the zig-zag overlap and interfere. Only at certain angles do they add up to a stable pattern that keeps its shape as it travels. Each such pattern is a mode.

A mode is described by its effective index neff: how fast it moves along the guide. It always lies between the cladding and core index. Flat bounces travel fast along the guide (high neff, close to the core index); steep bounces move slowly (low neff).

Make the core thinner. Higher modes disappear one by one until only mode 0 is left. Such a single-mode waveguide is what photonic chips typically use, because light then travels in one predictable way.

Notice the field reaching a little into the cladding. This evanescent tail will matter in the next chapter: it lets neighbouring waveguides exchange light.

In this idealized slab, mode 0 never disappears completely. In the wire waveguides used on chips it does: below a minimum size even mode 0 is cut off and the light is no longer guided, as the Waveguide Mode Solver shows. Later chapters call the modes TE0, TE1 and so on.

STEP 4 OF 4

See it in a wave simulation

Rays and formulas only go so far. Here the wave equation is solved on a grid, and you see the field from above. The core is a 300 nm wide silicon-like strip on glass.

Launch light. A beam that matches the mode's size couples in almost perfectly. A much wider or narrower beam only partly fits; the rest spreads out into the cladding and is lost. The light field in an optical fiber is much larger than the one in a waveguide on a chip (about 10 µm across, against well under 1 µm), which is why connecting a fiber to a chip needs special couplers.

Go around a bend. In a bend, part of the light hits the outer wall at a steeper angle than in a straight waveguide, closer to the perpendicular. Once that angle falls below the critical angle, the light is no longer totally reflected, so part of it leaks out and is lost. Tighter bends and weaker index contrast (a larger critical angle) lose more. That's why silicon allows micrometre-sized bends while low-contrast waveguides need millimetre ones.

What you've learned

Index decides direction

Light bends when the refractive index changes, and is totally reflected beyond the critical angle.

Core + cladding = waveguide

A high-index core inside lower-index cladding traps light by total internal reflection.

Modes

Only certain wave patterns fit. Chips typically use single-mode waveguides, described by their effective index.

Contrast sets the size

High index contrast confines light tightly and allows sharp bends: one reason silicon photonics can be so compact.

Model and accuracy. Steps 1 to 3 use exact formulas at 1550 nm: Snell's law, the Fresnel equations and the analytic modes of a slab waveguide.
