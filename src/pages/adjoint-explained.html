How Inverse Design Works

So far we have designed components by intuition, using what we know about how light is guided in waveguides. But what if we let the computer design them, based only on simulation results? That is inverse design. We state what the device should do, and an optimizer finds the shape. It can change thousands of pixels at once thanks to one trick, the adjoint method, which finds how every pixel affects the result from just two simulations. Seven steps show what it computes, how, and why it is allowed.

STEP 1 OF 7

Turn "a good device" into a number

The task is to bend light by 90° into the lower waveguide. Between the two waveguides sits a design region of 31 × 31 = 961 pixels. Each pixel can be cladding (glass) or core material.

To optimize, we need a single number that says how good a design is, the figure of merit (FOM). Here it is the fraction of the input power that arrives in the output waveguide's mode, measured at the detector (green line).

We start with an empty design region. Almost no light makes the turn.

FOM = |overlap of the field with the output mode|² ∈ [0, 1]
STEP 2 OF 7

The forward simulation

Light enters from the left. The simulation solves Maxwell's equations on a grid of cells. Every cell gives one equation linking its field to its neighbours, so the whole problem becomes one large linear system.

A(ε) · Efwd = b

A holds the material (the permittivity ε = n² of every cell) and the grid. Efwd is the forward field we want, and b is the source on the red line. One solve gives the field everywhere, and from it the FOM.

Note the waves spreading out of the input waveguide. Without a structure to guide it, most light misses the detector.

STEP 3 OF 7

The brute-force way: test every pixel

To improve the design we want the gradient. For every pixel, does adding material raise or lower the FOM, and by how much?

The obvious way is to change one pixel, simulate again, and compare. Tap pixels inside the dashed square to try it. Red pixels raised the FOM, blue ones lowered it.

That works, but every pixel costs a full simulation, and the optimizer needs the whole map again after every step.

STEP 4 OF 7

The adjoint simulation: send light back from the detector

The adjoint method needs only one more simulation. This time the source sits at the detector and sends light backwards into the device.

What it emits is the output mode, with an amplitude and phase set by how the FOM depends on the field there. In equations, its source is ∂FOM/∂Efwd. For a mode-overlap FOM, that is the output mode, phase-matched to the light that actually arrived.

Aᵀ · Eadj = (∂FOM/∂Efwd)ᵀ

Compare the two fields. The forward field Efwd shows where the light goes. The adjoint field Eadj shows where light would have to come from to reach the detector.

STEP 5 OF 7

The gradient is where the two fields overlap

Multiply the forward and adjoint fields cell by cell and you get the gradient for every cell at once.

∂FOM/∂εᵢ = −k₀²Δx² · Re( Eadj,i · Efwd,i )

Why does this work? Picture a tiny speck of extra material in one pixel. The forward light hits it, and the speck scatters a little light in all directions, like a small antenna. The question is whether this scattered light helps at the detector.

The adjoint field answers exactly that. Because light paths are reversible, Eadj at a pixel tells how light starting there would arrive at the detector, and with which phase. If the scattered light arrives in step with the light already there, the two add up and the FOM rises (red). If it arrives out of step, they cancel and the FOM falls (blue). The product of the two fields measures precisely this interference.

STEP 6 OF 7

Why are we allowed to do this?

Three facts about the physics make the trick exact, not an approximation.

The equations are linear in the field. The field depends on the material in a complicated way, but for a fixed material, A · Efwd = b is an ordinary linear system. That lets us differentiate it.

Each pixel changes only its own entry of A. Changing εᵢ touches one diagonal element, so its effect on the system is local and simple.

Light paths are reversible. For ordinary (reciprocal) materials A is symmetric, Aᵀ = A. The adjoint problem is the same physics with a different source, so the same solver solves it.

The cost no longer grows with the number of pixels. Whether 961 pixels or a million, the gradient always takes one forward and one adjoint simulation.

1
dFOM/dεᵢ = ∂FOM/∂Efwd · dEfwd/dεᵢ

The FOM depends on the material only through the field (chain rule; for complex fields, take the real part).

2
A·(dEfwd/dεᵢ) + (∂A/∂εᵢ)·Efwd = 0

Differentiate A·Efwd = b. The source b does not depend on the design.

3
dEfwd/dεᵢ = −A⁻¹ (∂A/∂εᵢ) Efwd

Brute force would solve this once per pixel, 961 times.

4
dFOM/dεᵢ = −[∂FOM/∂Efwd · A⁻¹] (∂A/∂εᵢ) Efwd = −Eadjᵀ (∂A/∂εᵢ) Efwd

Group the part that does not depend on i and call it Eadj. It is computed once, from Aᵀ Eadj = (∂FOM/∂Efwd)ᵀ. That is the adjoint simulation.

5
∂A/∂εᵢ = k₀²Δx² eᵢeᵢᵀ ⇒ dFOM/dεᵢ = −k₀²Δx² Eadj,i Efwd,i

Only cell i is touched, so the gradient is a product of the two fields in that cell (real part taken).

Same result, the Lagrange-multiplier way

Write L = FOM − Eadjᵀ(A·Efwd − b). Since A·Efwd = b holds, L = FOM for every design, so dL/dε = dFOM/dε. Choose Eadj so that all terms with dEfwd/dε cancel. That condition is Aᵀ Eadj = (∂FOM/∂Efwd)ᵀ. What remains is −Eadjᵀ(∂A/∂ε)Efwd. The adjoint field is the Lagrange multiplier that enforces Maxwell's equations.

STEP 7 OF 7

From gradient to design

Now repeat: forward simulation, adjoint simulation, gradient, and a small step for every pixel at once in the direction that raises the FOM. The design starts as uniform gray, a mixture halfway between core and cladding.

Two extra ingredients turn the result into something that can be fabricated. A filter blurs away tiny features, and a projection slowly forces every pixel to pure core or pure cladding. The gradient flows through both by the chain rule.

Watch panels 1 and 2 as the FOM approaches 100 %. They become almost identical. In a perfect device, light sent back from the detector retraces exactly the path the forward light takes. That is reciprocity at work.

The gradient only knows the local slope. The optimizer climbs the nearest hill, so different starting points can end at different designs.

In short

One number

Inverse design needs a figure of merit (FOM) that says what "good" means.

Two simulations

A forward simulation from the source and an adjoint simulation from the detector give the gradient for every pixel.

Exact, not approximate

Linearity, locality and reciprocity make the adjoint gradient equal to brute force, at a fraction of the cost.

Then climb

Small steps along the gradient, with filtering and projection, turn gray into a fabricable design.

Model and accuracy. 2D scalar model (E out of plane) at 1550 nm on an 80 nm grid: core n = 2.2, cladding n = 1.45, 400 nm wide waveguides and a 31 × 31-pixel design region.
